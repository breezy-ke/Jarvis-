01The thesis

A rally is a photograph. A register is a map.

A crowd cannot vote. A person at a polling station can.

Ten thousand people at a rally in Lodwar is a good afternoon and a good photograph. It tells you nothing you can act on: not who they are, not which ward they will queue in, not whether they were bussed from three constituencies away. On the morning of the vote that photograph does no work at all.

A verified register does. It says Kakuma has 12,153 registered voters and we can name 378 of them — so Kakuma is 3.1 per cent ours, which is thin, and here is the list of people there who already said they would help. That is not a slogan. That is Tuesday's route for the caravan.

The rule this whole system is built on Nothing is counted until the phone answers back. A signature on a paper list, a shout at a baraza, a follow on Facebook — none of it enters a published number. Only an SMS-verified supporter is a supporter. That discipline is what makes every figure on this page one we could defend in front of a journalist, a rival, or the party.

    06What one supporter is

    The record we hold —
    and the record we refuse to hold.

    Every field earns its place by answering a question the campaign actually has. What is missing is missing on purpose.

    Held, and used every day

    Seven fields

    • Full name — so a coordinator greets a real person, and near-duplicates inside a ward surface for review.
    • Verified phone — the proof. One person, one number. Masked in every export unless an admin pulls it.
    • Ward — the denominator. This is what turns a headcount into a percentage.
    • Polling station — where their vote is counted. A trigger rejects any station outside the ward they chose.
    • Chief's area — wards elect; sub-locations are how you convene a baraza. Kenya runs both, so we keep both.
    • Top issue — optional. The manifesto writes itself from evidence instead of instinct.
    • Pledge — optional. Everyone offering more than a vote becomes the ground-team list, by ward.
    Refused, by design

    Three refusals

    • Full national ID number — never stored. Last four digits only, and even that optional. We do not need it to know how many voters are ours.
    • Full date of birth — year only. Enough to segment youth turnout; not enough to impersonate anybody.
    • Raw IP and device data — hashed on the way in, never kept in the clear.

    Any opponent can promise to protect your data. We can show we structurally cannot leak what we never collected. When this campaign is accused of harvesting Turkana's identity numbers — and it will be — the answer is a schema, not a denial.

    07The advantage

    What they will know.
    What we will know.

    A well-run conventional campaign

    Runs on impressions

    • Crowd size at the last rally, estimated by eye.
    • Page likes — including from outside the county, and from people too young to vote.
    • What the mobilisers report, which is always what the principal wants to hear.
    • A paper list with no phone confirmation, no ward, and no way to test it.
    • On election eve: a number somebody made up, defended loudly.
    Akiyar

    Runs on the register

    • Every supporter tied to one of 559 polling stations — the same unit the results are declared in.
    • Saturation against the real IEBC denominator, so 604 in Kakuma reads differently from 604 in Kibish.
    • Duplicate and anomaly detection running continuously, so inflated numbers surface before a journalist finds them.
    • The top issue in every ward, in supporters' own words.
    • A ground-team list: everyone who offered to do more than vote, by ward, ready to call.

    The advantage is not that we are online and they are not — they will be online too. The advantage is that our number survives being questioned and theirs does not. In the last three weeks of a campaign, when every claim is contested and money must be spent in one place and not another, that is the only difference that pays.

    08What we need to start

    The first ninety days.

    In order, because two of these have long lead times we do not control and everything else waits on them.

    1. Start the shortcode paperworkWeek 1 Africa's Talking plus Communications Authority approval for a sender ID and USSD code. This is the longest pole in the tent and nothing about it speeds up later. Sandbox SMS works today, so testing is not blocked by it.
    2. Register with the ODPC as a Data ControllerWeek 1–2 Non-registration is itself an offence under the Data Protection Act. Alongside it: the privacy notice, the impact assessment, and a retention schedule that ends after the election is declared.
    3. Sign off the manifesto commitmentsWeek 2 Every promise on the site has to sit inside Article 96 — what a senator can actually deliver. Anything outside it is a line a rival will read back to us.
    4. Launch with three wards, not thirtyWeek 3–5 Lodwar Township, Kanamkemer and Kakuma. Highest handset density, so the digital funnel is testable fastest, and the real conversion rates replace the assumptions on this page.
    5. Recruit and train ward agentsWeek 4–9 Drawn from the ground-team list the register produces — people who already volunteered, in the ward they live in. Each sees only their own ward's supporters.
    6. Open the northWeek 6–13 Kibish, Lapur, Nanaam, Loima. USSD and baraza registration, because the digital funnel does not reach here — six of Kibish's ten polling stations are mobile or a water point — and the board should show that honestly rather than hiding it.
    One number to hold us to By day 90 the board should show no ward at zero. Not every ward warm — that takes two years. Every ward measured, so that from that point on, every shilling this campaign spends is aimed at a number instead of a hunch.
